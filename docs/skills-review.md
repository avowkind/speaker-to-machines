## Understanding AI

### AILT AI literacy (levels 1-4)

Understanding at a conceptual level how AI systems, including generative and agentic AI, work; what they can and cannot do; and how they fail.

- 1 Listener: Can explain in plain terms what AI is and what current systems, including generative and agentic AI, can and cannot do. Knows the common ways they fail: invented content, bias, inconsistency and overconfidence.
- 2 Caller: Recognises AI in the tools and services met day to day, and chooses sensibly whether to trust, check or avoid AI output in everyday situations.
- 3 Speaker: Applies a working understanding of AI to own decisions: judges which tasks suit AI, anticipates likely failure modes for a given use and explains AI behaviour to colleagues accurately and without hype or dismissal.
- 4 Shaper: Explains AI clearly to varied audiences and corrects common misconceptions. Builds others' literacy through teaching material, briefings or community discussion.

Examples (2026-09): UNESCO AI competency framework, EU–OECD AILit, AI Fluency courses

### MLFN Machine learning foundations (levels 1-5)

Understanding how models learn from data: training, validation, generalisation, overfitting, metrics and the main families of learning.

- 1 Listener: Knows that machine learning systems learn patterns from data rather than following written rules, and that their quality depends on the data and on how they are tested.
- 2 Caller: Understands the basic workflow of training data, a model, validation and predictions, and common terms such as features, labels, accuracy, overfitting and bias in data.
- 3 Speaker: Understands the main learning approaches (supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning), how models are evaluated and why results fail to generalise. Reads and critiques simple ML results and metrics with confidence.
- 4 Shaper: Applies ML understanding to design choices and critiques: problem framing, data suitability, choice of metric, leakage, uncertainty and the limits of what a model can show. Explains these to non-specialists.
- 5 Maker: Has the depth to guide ML work others do: reviews methods and results, spots flawed evaluation, and advises on approach and feasibility across a range of problems.

Examples (2026-09): scikit-learn, fast.ai, Kaggle

### FMIN Foundation model concepts (levels 1-6)

Understanding how large foundation models work and behave: architecture, tokens and context windows, sampling, embeddings, reasoning modes, multimodality, and the effects of scale and training data.

- 1 Listener: Knows that current AI assistants are built on large models trained on very large amounts of data to predict likely continuations, and that this explains both their fluency and their errors.
- 2 Caller: Understands practical concepts that affect use: context windows, tokens, training cut-off dates, randomness in outputs and the difference between the model and the tools around it.
- 3 Speaker: Understands how foundation models are built and adapted (pre-training, instruction and preference tuning, reasoning modes, multimodality, retrieval and tool use), and uses this to predict behaviour and choose approaches.
- 4 Shaper: Understands architecture and training well enough to explain trade-offs between model types and sizes, open and closed weights, and fine-tuning versus prompting or retrieval. Reads model documentation and technical reports critically.
- 5 Maker: Has deep technical understanding of model internals, training and scaling. Advises on technical direction and assesses new model claims and research results.
- 6 Keeper: Recognised as an authority on foundation models within an organisation or wider community. Shapes technical strategy on the basis of that understanding.

Examples (2026-09): transformer architecture, reasoning models, open-weight vs closed models

### AILS AI landscape awareness (levels 2-6)

Tracking AI models, vendors, capabilities and benchmarks, and separating credible evidence from hype when judging what is now possible.

- 2 Caller: Follows general news about AI and knows the main vendors, products and recent developments.
- 3 Speaker: Keeps up to date with capabilities relevant to own work: tracks model and product releases, tries new capabilities and separates demonstrated capability from marketing claims.
- 4 Shaper: Monitors the AI landscape for a team: evaluates new models and tools against real needs, maintains shared notes on what works and advises on when to adopt or wait.
- 5 Maker: Provides informed assessment of AI developments for decisions others make, drawing on primary sources such as technical reports, benchmarks and evaluations, and states uncertainty clearly.
- 6 Keeper: Advises organisational leadership on the direction of AI capability and its implications for strategy, investment and risk.

Examples (2026-09): model release notes, public benchmarks and leaderboards, system cards

### AETH AI ethics and societal impact (levels 1-7)

Recognising and acting on ethical issues raised by AI: fairness, bias, transparency, labour and environmental effects, and impacts on human autonomy.

- 1 Listener: Knows the main ethical issues raised by AI: fairness and bias, transparency, privacy, accountability, effects on work and on the environment, and misuse.
- 2 Caller: Recognises ethical issues in own use of AI and raises concerns through appropriate channels.
- 3 Speaker: Considers ethical impacts routinely in own AI work: who is affected, possible harm or unfairness, disclosure and consent. Adjusts or declines uses accordingly.
- 4 Shaper: Brings ethical analysis into team AI work through impact assessments, bias checks, stakeholder input and documented decisions. Helps others reason through difficult cases.
- 5 Maker: Designs ethical safeguards into AI systems and processes others rely on, including fairness testing, human oversight and redress. Reviews and challenges designs on ethical grounds.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational AI ethics principles and makes sure they are applied, with review processes, escalation and accountability.
- 7 Namer: Shapes ethical thinking and practice on AI beyond one organisation through research, public policy, standards or widely influential work.

### AREG AI law and regulation (levels 1-6)

Understanding and applying the law and regulation that governs AI development and use, including privacy, intellectual property, and AI-specific regulation across jurisdictions.

- 1 Listener: Knows that AI use is subject to law and regulation, including privacy, intellectual property, consumer protection and AI-specific rules, and that obligations differ by country.
- 2 Caller: Follows organisational rules derived from the law when using AI, and knows when to ask for advice.
- 3 Speaker: Identifies the legal and regulatory issues that apply to own AI work, such as personal data, copyright in inputs and outputs, disclosure duties and risk classification, and works within them.
- 4 Shaper: Applies AI law and regulation to team projects: interprets requirements, prepares documentation and impact assessments, and works with legal and compliance specialists.
- 5 Maker: Designs compliance into AI systems and processes others rely on: requirement mapping, technical documentation, conformity evidence and monitoring of regulatory change across jurisdictions.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational approach to AI regulation and compliance, including market strategy, accountability and engagement with regulators.

### ASAF AI safety concepts (levels 1-7)

Understanding risks from advanced AI and the methods used to reduce them: alignment, misuse prevention, evaluations for dangerous capabilities, and deployment safeguards.

- 1 Listener: Knows that advanced AI raises safety concerns beyond everyday errors, including misuse, loss of human control and systems pursuing unintended goals, and that developers use safeguards and evaluations to reduce them.
- 2 Caller: Understands common safety measures in deployed systems (refusals, usage policies, monitoring, staged releases) and why they exist.
- 3 Speaker: Understands the main safety approaches (alignment training, red-teaming, dangerous-capability evaluations, interpretability, control and monitoring) and applies safety thinking to own deployments of capable models and agents.
- 4 Shaper: Assesses safety risks of AI systems for a team, using published safety frameworks and system documentation, and designs appropriate safeguards and limits.
- 5 Maker: Leads safety work on AI systems others rely on, such as evaluations, safety cases, incident analysis and mitigations, and advises on deployment decisions.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational policy on AI safety, including thresholds for deployment, required evaluations and governance of high-capability systems.
- 7 Namer: Advances AI safety through research, standards, policy or widely adopted methods.

Examples (2026-09): frontier safety frameworks, system cards

## Working with AI

### INST Instructing AI (levels 1-6)

Communicating intent to AI systems: framing the task, supplying context, examples and constraints, and iterating to get useful results.

- 1 Listener: Knows that how a request is phrased, and what context is supplied, changes the result. Can write a simple request to a chat assistant and rephrase it when the answer misses.
- 2 Caller: Writes clear requests for routine tasks using examples, templates or advice from others. Adds basic context such as audience, format and length, and revises when results are poor.
- 3 Speaker: Routinely briefs AI systems for varied tasks in own work: states goal, context, constraints and what good looks like; supplies source material and examples; splits large requests into steps. Recognises when a task is poorly suited to AI and stops.
- 4 Shaper: Builds reusable instructions (system prompts, project instructions, templates, style guides) that give consistent results across tasks and for colleagues. Diagnoses failures such as missing context, ambiguity or conflicting instructions, and adapts technique to different models and modalities.
- 5 Maker: Designs the instruction and context strategy for AI products or team workflows, including structured outputs, testing prompt changes against a set of cases, and version control. Reviews others' instructions and sets team conventions.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational standards and shared libraries for instructing AI. Judges when to rely on instructions versus retrieval, fine-tuning or tooling across the organisation's AI portfolio.

Examples (2026-09): ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, project and custom instructions

### VERI Output verification (levels 1-5)

Critically evaluating AI output before relying on it: checking facts, sources, reasoning, code and figures, and matching the depth of checking to the consequences.

- 1 Listener: Knows that AI output can be fluent and wrong: invented facts, sources, figures or code that looks right. Knows that the person using it stays responsible for what they pass on.
- 2 Caller: Checks AI output against a known source or with help before using it. Spots obvious errors, missing references and answers that do not match the question.
- 3 Speaker: Routinely checks AI output in proportion to the stakes: follows citations to the source, re-derives key figures, runs and tests generated code, and asks for or seeks counter-evidence. Records what was checked when the output matters.
- 4 Shaper: Builds checking into repeatable workflows for self and team, such as checklists, second-model review, test cases and spot-audits. Recognises the failure patterns of particular task types and models, and teaches others to check effectively.
- 5 Maker: Designs verification for AI-assisted processes that other people rely on: which outputs need human review, sampling rates, acceptance criteria and escalation. Reviews incidents where unchecked output caused harm and changes practice.

Examples (2026-09): citation checking, cross-model comparison, running generated code and tests

### PRIV Information hygiene with AI (levels 1-5)

Protecting personal, client and confidential information when using AI: knowing what data goes where, how it is retained or trained on, and choosing settings and tools accordingly.

- 1 Listener: Knows that what is typed or uploaded to an AI service may be stored, reviewed or used for training, and that personal, client and confidential information needs care.
- 2 Caller: Follows given rules on what may be shared with which AI tools. Removes obvious personal or confidential details before use and uses the approved tool for work data.
- 3 Speaker: Chooses tools, plans and settings according to the sensitivity of the data: checks retention and training terms, uses organisational or local options for confidential work, limits connector access, and knows what agents can read on their behalf.
- 4 Shaper: Sets up safe defaults for self and team, such as approved tools by data class, redaction steps, account and connector settings, and guidance for new tools. Advises colleagues on specific cases.
- 5 Maker: Designs data-handling controls for AI use that others rely on: classification rules, technical controls on what reaches external models, logging and review. Works with privacy and security specialists on assessments.

Examples (2026-09): data retention settings, enterprise vs consumer plans, local models

## Delegating to AI agents

### DELG Task delegation (levels 1-6)

Deciding what work to hand to AI agents and how to break it down, split between agent-executable and human-reserved parts, and specify done.

- 1 Listener: Knows that AI agents can carry out multi-step tasks, not just answer questions, and that delegating well means deciding what to hand over and what to keep.
- 2 Caller: Hands simple, well-bounded tasks to an agent with a clear instruction and a clear definition of done, and reviews the result.
- 3 Speaker: Routinely delegates substantial work to agents: splits it into agent-suitable and human-reserved parts, specifies goals, constraints, inputs and acceptance criteria, sequences the work and integrates the results. Chooses the right kind of agent for the task.
- 4 Shaper: Designs repeatable delegation patterns for self and team, such as task templates, specifications, hand-off points and parallel work across several agents. Measures what works and moves the boundary between human and agent work as agents improve.
- 5 Maker: Designs how work is divided between people and agents across a team's processes, including roles, hand-offs, accountability and capacity planning. Sets delegation practice others follow.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational direction for delegating work to agents: which classes of work may be delegated, at what level of autonomy, and how this changes roles and accountability.

Examples (2026-09): agent modes in chat assistants, coding agents, browser agents

### SUPV Agent supervision (levels 1-6)

Keeping appropriate human control when AI acts on your behalf: setting permissions and limits, checking before acting on results, intervening, and reserving consequential decisions.

- 1 Listener: Understands that AI agents can take real actions (send, buy, change, delete) and that the person delegating stays accountable. Can name basic safeguards such as approval steps and undo.
- 2 Caller: Uses agents for low-risk actions with default safeguards on. Reviews proposed actions before approving them and stops an agent that goes off track.
- 3 Speaker: Decides what to delegate by how reversible and consequential it is. Sets permissions, spending and access limits. Checks evidence before relying on agent output. Recognises over-trust, scope creep and signs of manipulated input in own use.
- 4 Shaper: Designs supervision for recurring or unattended agent work (approval points, logs, checkpoints, rollback) for self and team. Investigates agent errors and adjusts delegation boundaries. Coaches others in safe delegation.
- 5 Maker: Defines oversight requirements for agentic systems other people depend on: autonomy by risk level, escalation conditions, audit trails and monitoring of agent behaviour. Reviews incidents and sets team practice.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational policy on agent autonomy and accountability, aligned with regulation and risk appetite. Accountable for oversight of agents operating at organisational scale.

Examples (2026-09): approval prompts, permission modes, audit logs

## Building with AI

### AISD AI-assisted software development (levels 1-6)

Using AI assistants and coding agents in the software development cycle: writing, explaining, reviewing, testing and refactoring code while owning its quality.

- 1 Listener: Knows AI can write, explain, review and test code, and knows the risks: plausible but wrong code, security flaws, licence issues and loss of understanding of one's own codebase.
- 2 Caller: Uses AI code completion and chat to write small pieces of code, explain unfamiliar code and fix errors, and reviews and runs everything before committing.
- 3 Speaker: Uses AI assistants and coding agents routinely across the development cycle (implementing features, writing tests, refactoring, debugging, documenting), keeping ownership of design and quality. Reviews generated changes as carefully as a colleague's and knows when to write code by hand.
- 4 Shaper: Shapes AI-assisted development for self and team: repository instruction files, conventions, reusable commands and skills, test-first and review practices that keep agent output reliable. Handles large changes such as migrations with agents while keeping them reviewable. Coaches other developers.
- 5 Maker: Designs development processes and tooling in which AI does a substantial share of the coding, including guardrails, review standards, security scanning, measures of quality and throughput, and how junior developers still learn. Sets engineering practice for a team or product.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational strategy for AI in software engineering: tools and vendors, policies on code provenance, licensing and security, effects on roles and hiring, and how productivity and quality are measured.

Examples (2026-09): Claude Code, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, OpenAI Codex

Organising software delivery around agents: spec-driven development, parallel agents, agents in CI/CD, and review and merge controls for agent-produced change.

- 5 Maker: Designs agent-driven delivery pipelines other people rely on, such as automated migrations, dependency and vulnerability remediation, or change applied across many repositories, with specifications, verification, staged rollout, rollback and audit.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational practice for agent-driven software delivery: which changes may be agent-authored, required controls and evidence, platform choices, and how accountability for shipped code is kept.
- 7 Namer: Advances how the industry delivers software with agents through widely adopted methods, tools, platforms or published practice.

- 5 Maker: Designs and builds capability servers and skill libraries used by other people or products. Handles authentication, permissions, versioning, error behaviour and threats such as tool poisoning and injection. Evaluates capability quality systematically.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational architecture and standards for agent capabilities: catalogue, review and approval, security policy and reuse. Decides whether to build or adopt across vendors.
- 7 Namer: Contributes to public protocols, standards or widely adopted capability ecosystems, and influences how agent capabilities are designed across the industry.

### EVAL AI evaluation (levels 2-7)

- 3 Speaker: Builds evaluation sets for own AI features from real examples, defines pass criteria, uses automated and model-based grading with spot checks by people, and compares prompts or models on evidence.
- 4 Shaper: Makes evaluation routine for a team: regression suites run on every change, metrics tied to user outcomes, calibrated model-based graders, red-team cases and evaluation of agents on multi-step tasks.
- 5 Maker: Designs evaluation strategy for AI systems others rely on, covering offline and online measures, human review programmes, safety and bias testing, and release criteria. Judges what the evidence does and does not show.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational standards for AI evaluation and release decisions, including required evidence, independent review and reporting to governance.
- 7 Namer: Advances evaluation science or practice through new methods, widely used benchmarks or tools, or published research.

Examples (2026-09): eval harnesses, LLM-as-judge, agent task benchmarks

### AISC AI security (levels 2-7)

Protecting AI systems and their users from attack and misuse: prompt injection, data exfiltration, tool and supply-chain risk, and permission design.

- 2 Caller: Knows the main attacks on AI systems (prompt injection, data leakage, poisoned tools or content, jailbreaks) and follows secure-use guidance for agents and connectors.
- 3 Speaker: Builds AI features with basic defences: least-privilege tools and credentials, separation of untrusted content from instructions, output handling, sandboxed execution and review of third-party skills, plugins and servers.
- 4 Shaper: Tests and hardens AI systems for a team through threat modelling, red-teaming for injection and exfiltration, monitoring for misuse and supply-chain checks on models, packages and agent capabilities. Advises colleagues.
- 5 Maker: Designs security architecture for AI systems others rely on: identity and permissions for agents, isolation, data-flow controls, detection and incident response. Sets team security practice for AI.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational AI security strategy and policy, integrating it with the wider security programme, risk appetite and regulation.
- 7 Namer: Advances AI security through research, widely used tools, frameworks or standards.

Examples (2026-09): OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications, sandboxing, MITRE ATLAS

### LOPS AI operations and observability (levels 3-6)

Running AI systems in production: tracing, monitoring, cost and latency management, incident handling and change control for models and prompts.

- 3 Speaker: Monitors AI features in production (errors, latency, cost and quality signals), traces individual requests and agent runs, and investigates reported failures.
- 4 Shaper: Runs AI systems reliably for a team: tracing and dashboards, alerting on quality and cost, controlled roll-out of prompt and model changes, incident handling and capacity planning with providers.
- 5 Maker: Designs operations for AI systems others rely on: observability architecture, service levels, change control for models and prompts, cost governance and resilience to provider outages or model changes.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational standards and platforms for operating AI in production, and is accountable for reliability, cost and incident management at scale.

### MSEL Model selection (levels 2-6)

Choosing models and deployment options for a purpose on evidence: capability, cost, latency, privacy, licensing and vendor risk.

- 2 Caller: Chooses between available models or modes for own tasks, based on guidance about their strengths, cost and data terms.
- 3 Speaker: Selects models for a use case on evidence: runs comparisons on own tasks, weighs quality, speed, cost, context size, data handling and licence, and revisits the choice when new models appear.
- 4 Shaper: Maintains model choices for a team or product: evaluation-based comparisons, routing between models, fallbacks and tracking of price and capability changes. Advises others.

## Physical and embodied AI

### PERC Machine perception (levels 1-7)

Applying AI to interpret sensor data (camera, lidar, radar, audio) for detection, tracking, mapping and scene understanding.

- 1 Listener: Knows that AI can interpret images, video, sound and other sensor data to recognise objects, people, speech and scenes, and knows the main limits and risks, including errors in poor conditions, bias and surveillance.
- 2 Caller: Uses perception features in everyday tools and devices, such as image search, transcription, camera recognition and scanning, and checks results where they matter.
- 3 Speaker: Applies existing perception models to own tasks (detection, classification, segmentation, transcription or tracking), prepares test data and measures accuracy in the conditions that matter.
- 4 Shaper: Builds perception systems for real environments: adapts or trains models, fuses multiple sensors, handles lighting, weather, occlusion and edge cases, and validates performance against requirements.
- 5 Maker: Designs perception architectures others rely on, including safety-relevant ones, balancing accuracy, latency, sensor cost and robustness, and leads validation.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational strategy for machine perception, including sensor choices, data, platforms and assurance.
- 7 Namer: Advances machine perception through research, widely used models or datasets, or standards.

Examples (2026-09): vision-language models, YOLO, SLAM

### EDGE Edge and IoT AI (levels 2-6)

Running AI on constrained devices and sensor networks, balancing accuracy, power, latency and connectivity.

- 2 Caller: Sets up ready-made AI features on edge devices or sensors by following instructions, and checks they work in place.
- 3 Speaker: Deploys AI models to constrained devices (single-board computers, cameras, microcontrollers), handles connectivity and power limits and monitors them in the field.
- 4 Shaper: Builds edge AI solutions: optimises models for size and speed, manages fleets of devices, updates them securely and combines local and cloud processing sensibly.
- 5 Maker: Designs edge and IoT AI systems others rely on, balancing accuracy, power, latency, cost, privacy and maintainability over the device lifetime.

### ROBO Robotics and embodied AI (levels 1-7)

Applying AI to machines that sense and act in the physical world: perception, planning, control and learning for robots, with attention to physical safety.

- 1 Listener: Understands how AI-driven robots differ from fixed automation (sensing, uncertainty, learning) and the main safety and social issues. Can describe current applications such as warehouse, domestic, surgical and humanoid robots.
- 2 Caller: Operates or supervises AI-enabled robots or consumer robotic devices under defined procedures. Follows safety rules and reports faults or unexpected behaviour.
- 3 Speaker: Configures, programs and tunes robotic systems using existing AI components (perception models, planners, simulation) for defined tasks. Tests in simulation before deployment and diagnoses common perception and control failures.
- 4 Shaper: Integrates perception, planning and control into working robotic applications. Adapts or trains models for new tasks or environments, uses simulation and real-world data to close the gap between them, and applies relevant safety standards.
- 5 Maker: Designs robotic AI systems other people rely on, balancing capability, safety, cost and reliability. Leads validation and safety cases and sets team engineering practice.
- 6 Keeper: Sets organisational strategy for robotics and embodied AI. Accountable for safety assurance and deployment at scale.
- 7 Namer: Advances the field through research, widely used platforms, robot foundation models or safety standards.

Examples (2026-09): ROS 2, NVIDIA Isaac, vision-language-action models, domestic and warehouse robots

### AUTV Autonomous vehicles and mobility (levels 1-7)

- 7 Namer: Advances autonomous mobility through research, industry standards or widely adopted platforms.
